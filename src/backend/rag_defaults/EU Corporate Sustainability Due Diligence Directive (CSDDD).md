# EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (CSDDD)

Reference document for classifying labour and human-rights due-diligence obligations under EU law. It reflects the **consolidated text after the 2025–2026 "Omnibus" simplification**, in force since 18 March 2026. Where a figure changed with the Omnibus, that is noted.

## Instrument

- Directive (EU) 2024/1760 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 13 June 2024 on corporate sustainability due diligence (CSDDD / CS3D). Original text in force 25 July 2024.
- Amended by the "Omnibus I" simplification package, in force 18 March 2026 (published in the Official Journal 26 February 2026). The figures below reflect the amended text.
- Primary source: Directive (EU) 2024/1760, EUR-Lex.

## Companies covered (scope)

| Category | Threshold |
|---|---|
| EU companies | More than 5,000 employees **and** more than EUR 1.5 billion net worldwide turnover in the last financial year |
| Non-EU companies | More than EUR 1.5 billion net turnover generated in the EU |
| Groups | Thresholds are also assessed on a consolidated basis at the level of the ultimate parent |
| SMEs | Micro, small and medium enterprises are **not** directly covered; they may be affected indirectly as business partners of covered companies |

Obligations apply at the level of the company and its group (including subsidiaries), **not** at the level of an individual site. Whether a specific company is covered is a fact to be established case by case; a violation at a site that is not itself in scope may still fall within the chain of activities of an in-scope company.

## Core obligation: risk-based human-rights and environmental due diligence

Covered companies must identify, prevent, mitigate, bring to an end, and account for adverse human-rights and environmental impacts across:
- their own operations and those of their subsidiaries; and
- their **chain of activities** — upstream business partners (design, extraction, sourcing, manufacture, transport, storage of raw materials or components) and specified downstream activities (distribution, transport, storage carried out for the company).

Due diligence steps:
1. Integrate due diligence into policies and risk-management systems.
2. Identify and assess actual and potential adverse impacts (map operations, subsidiaries and partners; then assess in depth the areas where impacts are most likely and severe).
3. Prevent and mitigate potential impacts; bring actual impacts to an end or minimise them.
4. Provide remediation for actual adverse impacts the company caused or contributed to.
5. Carry out meaningful engagement with stakeholders.
6. Operate a notification mechanism and complaints procedure (see below).
7. Monitor the effectiveness of due diligence measures.
8. Publicly communicate on due diligence.

**Omnibus changes to the due-diligence obligation:** in-depth assessment is prioritised on **direct business partners**; indirect partners are examined only where plausible information indicates an adverse impact at that level. Information requests to smaller partners must be targeted, reasonable and proportionate. The obligation to terminate a business relationship as a last resort was removed: continuing a relationship under a reinforced prevention plan, where there is a reasonable expectation it will work, does not by itself expose the company to penalty or liability.

## Human rights covered (Annex, Part I) — mapped to source instruments

The directive defines adverse human-rights impacts by reference to rights and prohibitions drawn from international instruments. Each is interpreted in line with the instrument cited. Grouped by subject (the level at which this tool classifies):

**Freedom of association and collective bargaining** — the right to freedom of association and assembly, the right to organise, the right to collective bargaining, and **the right to strike**. Interpreted in line with ICCPR Arts 21–22, ICESCR Art 8, ILO C87 and C98. Includes the right to form and join a union, protection from discrimination or retaliation for union membership, and the right of unions to operate freely.

**Forced labour** — prohibition of forced or compulsory labour, including debt bondage (any work exacted under threat of penalty and not offered voluntarily). Interpreted in line with ILO C29 (and its 2014 Protocol), ILO C105, and ICCPR Art 8.

**Slavery and trafficking** — prohibition of slavery, servitude, slavery-like practices and human trafficking. ICCPR Art 8.

**Child labour** — prohibition of employing a child below the age of completing compulsory schooling and in any case below 15 (ILO C138); prohibition of the worst forms of child labour for persons under 18 (ILO C182); children's rights to health, education, an adequate standard of living and protection from economic, labour and sexual exploitation (CRC Arts 24, 27, 28, 32, 34, 35).

**Discrimination in employment** — prohibition of unequal treatment in employment unless justified by the requirements of the role, including unequal pay for work of equal value. ILO C100 and C111; ICESCR Art 7.

**Just and favourable conditions of work** — a fair wage and an adequate **living wage** for employees (and an adequate living income for self-employed workers and smallholders), a decent living, safe and healthy working conditions, and reasonable limitation of working hours. ICESCR Arts 7 and 11.

**Basic workplace provision** — prohibition of restricting workers' access to adequate housing where the employer provides it, and to adequate food, water and sanitation in the workplace. ICESCR Art 11.

**Life and physical integrity** — the right to life (ICCPR Art 6(1)); its abuse expressly includes the case of private or public security personnel protecting the company's resources, facilities or personnel causing a person's death through a lack of instruction or control by the company. Prohibition of torture and cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment (ICCPR Art 7).

**Other civil rights** — right to liberty and security (ICCPR Art 9(1)); prohibition of arbitrary or unlawful interference with privacy, family, home or correspondence (ICCPR Art 17); freedom of thought, conscience and religion (ICCPR Art 18).

**Environment and land** — prohibition of causing measurable environmental degradation (soil, water or air contamination, harmful emissions, excessive water use, deforestation) that impairs the enjoyment of rights (food, water, health, property). Prohibition of unlawful eviction and of the unlawful taking of land, forests and waters on which people's livelihood depends; rights of indigenous peoples and communities over their lands and resources.

**Residual clause (Art 3)** — any other right in the listed instruments also counts as an adverse impact where the right can be abused by a company, the abuse directly affects a legally protected interest, and the company could reasonably have foreseen the risk.

**Reference instruments (Annex Part I, Section 2):** Universal Declaration of Human Rights; ICCPR; ICESCR; UN Convention on the Rights of the Child; and the ILO fundamental Conventions — C29 (forced labour, 1930) and its 2014 Protocol, C87 (freedom of association), C98 (collective bargaining), C100 (equal remuneration), C105 (abolition of forced labour), C111 (discrimination), C138 (minimum age), C182 (worst forms of child labour). The occupational safety and health Conventions C155 and C187 are to be added to the Annex once ratified by all Member States.

## Mapping to the ILO Fundamental Principles and Rights at Work (1998, expanded 2022)

- Freedom of association and effective recognition of collective bargaining → C87 / C98 cluster.
- Elimination of all forms of forced or compulsory labour → C29 / C105 cluster.
- Effective abolition of child labour → C138 / C182 cluster.
- Elimination of discrimination in respect of employment and occupation → C100 / C111 cluster.
- A safe and healthy working environment → just-and-favourable-conditions cluster (and C155 / C187 once formally added to the Annex).

## Complaints and notification mechanism (Art 14)

Covered companies must provide an accessible, fair and transparent complaints procedure open to: persons affected or with reasonable grounds to believe they may be affected, and their legitimate representatives (including civil-society organisations and human-rights defenders); **trade unions and other workers' representatives** representing workers in the chain of activities concerned; and civil-society organisations active in the field where the subject is an environmental impact. Complainants are entitled to follow-up, to meet company representatives, and to a reasoned response; the company must protect confidentiality and prevent retaliation. A substantiated complaint counts as an identified impact and triggers the prevention / remediation measures.

## Enforcement

- Each Member State designates one or more supervisory authorities with powers to investigate, order corrective action and injunctions, and impose effective, proportionate and dissuasive penalties.
- Administrative penalties: the maximum limit is set at **3% of the company's net worldwide turnover** for the most serious infringements (reduced from 5%).
- Civil liability: there is **no longer a harmonised EU civil-liability regime**; liability for damage caused by a failure to comply is governed by national law. Where a company is liable under national law for damage caused by an intentional or negligent failure, affected persons are entitled to full compensation.

## Review frequency

The effectiveness of due-diligence measures is reviewed **at least every 5 years** (previously annual), and whenever there are reasonable grounds to believe the measures are no longer adequate.

## Climate transition plan

Companies must adopt a transition plan for climate-change mitigation. The Omnibus removed the separate standalone obligation to "put the plan into effect" as an autonomous legal duty.

## Provenance and scope of this summary

Consolidated summary of Directive (EU) 2024/1760 as amended by the Omnibus I package. Rights coverage follows Annex Part I and is organised here by subject and source instrument rather than by the Annex's internal point numbers (the authoritative verbatim numbered Annex is the EUR-Lex text). No source consulted indicates that the 2025–2026 reform changed the substantive list of covered rights in Annex Part I or Part II; the reform changed the scope of application, the depth of chain-of-activities assessment, the review frequency, the climate-plan and civil-liability provisions, and the penalty cap — not the list of covered rights.
