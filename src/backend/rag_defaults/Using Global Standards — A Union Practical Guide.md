# Using Global Standards — A Union Practical Guide

Practical reference for connecting a worker's situation to international frameworks, documenting it well, and escalating it through the right channels. It complements the framework reference documents (ILO, OECD, UNGPs, CSDDD).

## The frameworks, in practice

- **ILO fundamental principles and rights at work** are the floor: freedom of association and collective bargaining, elimination of forced labour, abolition of child labour, elimination of discrimination, and a safe and healthy working environment. They carry the widest recognition and the most complaint avenues.
- **UN Guiding Principles (UNGPs)** set the corporate responsibility to respect human rights and the expectation of human-rights due diligence, independent of state action.
- **OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises** turn that responsibility into due-diligence expectations for multinationals and provide a non-judicial grievance mechanism through National Contact Points (NCPs) — one of the few avenues open to unions.
- **EU CSDDD** makes risk-based due diligence a legal obligation for the largest companies across their chain of activities, with enforcement and civil liability under national law.

## Company commitments as leverage

Codes of conduct, sustainability reports, and public commitments are not legally binding in themselves, but they are acknowledged obligations the company has made publicly. They matter because:
- they establish the company's own stated standard, which can be held up against its conduct;
- mandatory due-diligence law increasingly gives them teeth;
- they are concrete tools in dialogue with management and in campaigns.

Always record the exact commitment and its source.

## Spotting and identifying violations

- Start from what the worker describes; connect it to the fundamental principle it engages before reaching for secondary indicators.
- Common reasons violations go unreported: fear of retaliation, not recognising conduct as a rights violation, distrust of the process. Acknowledge these.
- Common documentation mistakes: vague accounts without dates or names, no evidence preserved, conflating opinion with fact, and over-stating (which weakens credibility).

## Documentation and evidence standards

- Capture **who, what, when, where**, the **duration** of the issue, and **who in the company knew and when** (local management vs headquarters).
- Distinguish **facts the worker stated** from **inferences**. Preserve short **verbatim quotes** where they carry weight.
- For each action taken, record a **document reference, a short summary, the date, and the status** (resolved / pending / no response).
- Evidence quality ladder: documentary evidence (communications, notices, contracts, photographs, medical/OSH records) is strongest; corroborated witness testimony next; a single uncorroborated account is weakest. Note gaps honestly rather than filling them.
- Protect the confidentiality and safety of workers and witnesses; never publish identifying detail without consent.

## Escalation

- The path is always: **worker → national trade union → UNI Global Union (or the relevant Global Union Federation)**. This tool prepares documentation; the unions decide and act.
- Do not advise contacting international bodies (ILO, OECD National Contact Points, certification bodies) directly.
- **When to escalate:** a clear fundamental-rights violation with evidence; a multinational or cross-border dimension; a pattern across sites; or where national avenues are exhausted or unsafe.
- **What strengthens an escalation:** a complete chronology, preserved evidence, the corporate structure (parent company, buyers, investors), and the company's own public commitments.

## Coordination structures

- **European Works Councils (EWCs)** can raise issues at group level and are relevant where the company operates across the EU.
- **Trade Union Alliances (TUAs)** coordinate pressure across a multinational's sites and countries.
- **Global Framework Agreements (GFAs)** between a company and a Global Union Federation create an agreed basis for dialogue and dispute resolution; where one exists, it is a primary channel.
