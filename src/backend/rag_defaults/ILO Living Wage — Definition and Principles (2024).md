# ILO Living Wage — Definition and Principles (2024)

Reference document. A wage below a living wage is a **related labour-standards concern**, not in itself a violation of a fundamental principle. It reinforces documentation and may strengthen specific processes; follow it only when it arises in the user's own account.

## Status

Not a Convention. A **definition and set of principles** agreed at the ILO Meeting of Experts on Wage Policies (Geneva, 19–23 February 2024) and endorsed by the ILO Governing Body on 13 March 2024. This is the first time the ILO has formally defined a living wage and set principles for estimating one.

## Agreed definition

> A living wage is "the wage level that is necessary to afford a decent standard of living for workers and their families, taking into account the country circumstances and calculated for the work performed during the normal hours of work."

## Principles for estimation

- Grounded in the concept of a **decent standard of living** for workers and their families.
- Estimated using **robust data** and a **transparent, sound methodology**.
- Determined through **social dialogue**, with the participation of workers' and employers' organisations.
- Take account of both the needs of workers and their families and relevant economic factors.
- **Country- and context-specific**; adjusted over time.
- Realised progressively through national wage-setting mechanisms, including minimum wages and collective bargaining — the living wage complements, and is generally higher than, a statutory minimum wage.

## Relationship to other standards

- Connects to the right to just and favourable conditions of work, including fair remuneration providing a decent living (ICESCR Art 7).
- The EU CSDDD Annex expressly covers "an adequate living wage for employees and an adequate living income for self-employed workers and smallholders."
- FSC standards include a living-wage criterion for certified companies.

## Relevance for classification

Below-living-wage pay is documented where it arises. It can reinforce a broader case (e.g. alongside coercion it may point toward forced-labour indicators) but is not, on its own, a fundamental-rights violation and is not an investigative target of the interview.
