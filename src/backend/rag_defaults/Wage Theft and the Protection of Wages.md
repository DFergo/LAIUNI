# Wage Theft and the Protection of Wages

Reference document. "Wage theft" is a **related labour-standards concern**. It reinforces documentation and, in combination with coercion, can be an indicator of forced labour; otherwise it is followed only when it arises in the user's own account.

## Concept

"Wage theft" is not a formal ILO instrument by that name. It describes the **non-payment or underpayment of wages and benefits that are legally or contractually owed** to workers. It is grounded in ILO Convention No. 95 (Protection of Wages, 1949) and Recommendation No. 85.

## Common forms

- Non-payment of wages for work performed.
- Underpayment below the statutory minimum or the agreed rate.
- Unpaid overtime or unpaid working time.
- Unlawful or excessive deductions (for tools, equipment, accommodation, transport, or recruitment fees).
- Withholding of final pay on termination.
- Non-payment of legally required social contributions or benefits.
- Persistent late payment.

## ILO Convention 95 — core principles

- Wages must be paid **regularly, in full and directly to the worker**, in legal tender.
- **Deductions** are permitted only within limits set by law, collective agreement or arbitration award.
- Employers may not limit a worker's freedom to dispose of their wages, nor use company stores or wage practices to coerce workers.
- Wages must be protected in the event of employer insolvency.
- Methods of wage payment that deprive workers of a genuine opportunity to terminate employment are prohibited.

## Connection to forced labour

Withholding of wages, unlawful wage deductions and debt bondage are recognised **indicators of forced labour** when used to bind or coerce workers. Where wage withholding is coupled with coercion, menace of penalty, or restriction of movement, it may point toward a forced-labour situation (ILO C29) rather than a stand-alone wage dispute.

## Relevance for classification

Maps to ILO C95. Document where it arises. Escalate the analysis toward forced labour only where the surrounding facts (coercion, debt, restriction of freedom) support it; otherwise treat it as a related concern that reinforces the case.
